Tokenization is the first step in almost every natural language processing
pipeline, yet it is often the least discussed part of how large language
models actually work. Before a model can predict the next word in a
sentence, the raw text has to be broken down into smaller units called
tokens, and each token has to be mapped to an integer that the model's
embedding table understands. The choices made at this stage ripple through
everything downstream: how many tokens a sentence needs, how well the
model generalizes to rare words, and even how fairly the model treats
different languages.

Early tokenizers were simple. A whitespace tokenizer just splits text on
spaces, treating punctuation as part of whatever word it happens to
touch. A character-level tokenizer goes to the other extreme, treating
every single character as its own token, which guarantees there is never
an unknown symbol but also means sentences turn into very long sequences
of tokens. Neither approach scales well to a vocabulary of hundreds of
thousands of words, and neither one handles a word the tokenizer has
never seen before in a satisfying way.

Subword tokenization was designed to sit between these two extremes. Byte
Pair Encoding starts from individual characters and repeatedly merges the
most frequent adjacent pair of symbols, building up a vocabulary of
common word pieces without ever needing a fixed dictionary of whole
words. A word like "unbelievable" might end up split into pieces such as
"un", "believ", and "able" if those pieces were frequent enough during
training, letting the tokenizer reconstruct rare or unseen words out of
familiar fragments rather than falling back to a single unknown token.

WordPiece, used by BERT and its many descendants, follows a similar idea
but changes the criterion for choosing which pair to merge next. Instead
of picking the single most frequent pair, it favors pairs that are
frequent relative to how often their individual pieces occur on their
own, which tends to avoid merging extremely common but uninformative
combinations. The practical effect is a different, though often similar
looking, vocabulary than plain frequency-based BPE would produce on the
same corpus.

Modern production tokenizers, such as the byte-level BPE used by GPT-style
models, add one more twist: instead of operating on Unicode characters,
they operate directly on the underlying bytes of the UTF-8 encoding. This
means there are only two hundred fifty six possible base symbols, so the
tokenizer never encounters a truly unknown character, even for scripts or
emoji it never saw during training. The tradeoff is that a single visible
character can sometimes be split across more than one token when its
byte sequence does not line up neatly with a previously learned merge.

None of these differences are purely academic. A tokenizer trained
mostly on English text will typically need noticeably more tokens to
represent the same sentence in Japanese or Korean than a tokenizer
trained on a balanced multilingual corpus, simply because it never
learned efficient merges for those scripts. This is one of the reasons
comparing tokenizers fairly requires reporting vocabulary size and
training corpus alongside any compression ratio, rather than treating a
single number as a verdict on which algorithm is objectively better.
